Morning Hugh. Here's your market news, and since it's Saturday, these are Friday's closes.

Rates first. The US ten-year was up just over a basis point to five point two four percent. The Swiss ten-year went the other way, down nearly two basis points to fifty-six basis points. SOFR fixed a basis point lower at three point eight seven, and €STR was unchanged at two point four four. Nothing out of the ordinary there.

Equities had a good day. The Dow led, up point eight percent to just over fifty-one thousand six hundred and fifty, which is about a one standard deviation move, so a solid session rather than a dramatic one. The S&P 500 added point six percent to seven thousand eight hundred and eleven, and the Nasdaq was up point six as well. Lower oil prices gave some relief after a week of rising energy costs and bond yields, and stocks bounced back from Thursday's tech-led selloff to finish the week up, not far off record highs. MSCI World was up point six, MSCI Emerging Markets point six, and the Euro Stoxx 50 point eight. Gold spot rose about one and a half percent to around four thousand one hundred and ninety dollars, which sounds big but is a normal day for gold this year. Euro-dollar was flat at one point one two.

Now the central banks, and this is the part worth watching. The next Fed decision is on Wednesday the twenty-eighth of October. Fed funds futures are pricing roughly an eighteen percent chance of a quarter-point hike there. By the December meeting, on the ninth, the market has close to a full hike priced in, which would take the rate to about four point one four percent. The September minutes came out this week, and the New York Fed's survey shows one-year inflation expectations at three point nine percent, the highest since May twenty twenty-three. Separately, New York Fed researchers found tariffs added nearly three percentage points to inflation across sixty-seven categories of goods.

The ECB meets the day after the Fed, on Thursday the twenty-ninth. The deposit rate is two and a half percent, and markets see a hold as very likely, with only a small chance of a hike priced. No major US or euro area data came out in the last couple of days.

Now the market news. The theme in the papers this weekend is concentration risk. The FT argues investor portfolios have become alarmingly dependent on one trade, the AI build-out, and that slowing deal activity is another warning sign, with rising yields and AI jitters hitting appetite for risk. In credit, high-yield spreads have been widening, and margin debt is at a record as retail investors borrow against their portfolios. On the other side of that trade, options markets expect Nvidia to cross six trillion dollars in market value before the end of the month.

Energy is the other big story. Hurricane Isaias has disrupted oil and gas production as it moved towards the Gulf, and prediction markets on Kalshi have petrol staying above four dollars a gallon through election day on the third of November. In Washington, Treasury Secretary Bessent has hired Judy Shelton as an adviser. She's the former Fed nominee the Senate blocked over her economic views. In fintech, Blockchain dot com has applied to the CFTC to offer prediction markets and crypto derivatives in the US, and Nasdaq's chief executive Adena Friedman says tokenisation could free up tens of billions of dollars in trapped capital. Finally, S&P thinks China's property market could start to turn next year, beginning with the largest cities.

To the US. The big story is Trump's deal for Russia to supply diesel. It eases sanctions on Moscow to bring fuel prices down before the midterms next month, and critics say it contradicts the Russia sanctions law. Zelensky called it an investment in a war that should be ending, not prolonged. Hurricane Isaias has since been downgraded after landfall in Florida, but hundreds of thousands of homes in Florida and Alabama have lost power. The White House has picked Katie Zacharia, a communications adviser at Trump Media, as its new press secretary. And Trump went after the Nobel committee in a Truth Social post after missing out on the Peace Prize, saying he should have won eight.

Over to Europe. The most interesting one for us is the EU budget. Ireland holds the Council presidency, and the Irish proposal cuts the EU's seven-year budget by around a hundred and forty billion euro compared with the Commission's plan, though Politico puts it nearer a hundred and sixty. The cuts fall on competitiveness and development aid, while farm and regional funding are spared. EU finance ministers have also reached a deal on a key part of the Savings and Investment Union, the plan to deepen Europe's capital markets. In Germany, fifty thousand jobs at Volkswagen are under threat, which has become a symbol of the wider industrial slump. Turkey has banned social media for children under fifteen. France is offering three thousand extra teachers to calm student protests. In Ukraine, Russian strikes on Zaporizhzhia killed at least eleven people, three of them children, and the attacks on the power grid have some people in Kyiv packing up before winter. Ukraine has hit back at Russia's tech sector, with drone strikes knocking out a Yandex data centre used to train its AI models. In the UK, Prime Minister Burnham has promised to curb non-compete clauses in job contracts, and a former Deutsche Bank trader jailed for rigging interest rates has had his conviction overturned.

Now home, in a bit more detail. The big domestic story is Wednesday's public sector strike. The money for a pay deal is in the Budget, and both sides say they want an agreement, but it now looks close to inevitable. The Dáil business committee met in an emergency session to decide whether to cancel or move Wednesday's sitting rather than have TDs cross picket lines. On the Budget itself, the ESRI says the changes to tax, welfare and childcare add up to only small income gains for most households.

On housing, the Land Development Agency is under scrutiny on two fronts. Staff there shared two million euro in bonuses, which the agency says it needs to compete with the private sector for workers. Separately, its chief executive has defended paying around two million euro for a bungalow beside the old Central Mental Hospital site in Dundrum, saying it was a pragmatic move to get the site built on and not ransom money.

In business, Michael O'Leary says airlines are now in a full-blown crisis over fuel costs. He's warned before that two or three weaker European carriers could fail this winter if oil stays where it is, and that fares may have to rise by ten to twenty percent. The fuel squeeze is reaching retail too: Eason's online business took a hit from higher fuel costs, and profits fell despite higher revenue. Kenmare Resources, the Irish-listed miner that runs the Moma titanium mine in Mozambique, confirmed a takeover approach from Abu Dhabi's International Resources Holding this week. The shares jumped around forty percent, and IRH has until the seventeenth of November to make a firm offer. In Waterford, residents and farmers are protesting a big data centre and energy development planned for Kilmeaden, another sign of the pushback against data centres here. The ISEQ ended the week lower, even as global stocks rose.

Up north, the Northern Ireland Secretary, Chris Bryant, overruled the Parades Commission and banned the Orange Order's march along the Garvaghy Road in Portadown after a thirteen-day standoff. The Orange Order has gone to court to challenge it. In sport, the FAI says it has found inconsistencies in the Garda vetting process, with up to sixty League of Ireland coaches still waiting for approval.

Finally, AI and software, and there's a lot this week. The standout for me is a new open-source tool from Anthropic called REA, short for Reverse Engineer Anything. It lets AI coding agents take apart a compiled program, whether that's a native binary, a JavaScript app or a browser game, and explain in plain English how it works. That used to be slow, specialist work, and it's MIT-licensed, so anyone can use it. It was one of the most upvoted posts on Hacker News this week.

On the research side, OpenAI has released seven hundred and twenty-two AI-written maths papers from an unreleased model, covering more than a hundred open problems. Mathematicians have called it staggering and overwhelming, and some are sceptical. Around forty percent of the headline results come with proofs checked by computer in the Lean proof assistant, but nobody has worked through most of them yet.

Money is still pouring in. Typesafe, the start-up behind Jev, a model that doesn't work with text the way language models do, raised eight hundred and seventy million dollars at a seven and a half billion dollar valuation, weeks after launch. Its pitch is that it's faster and uses far fewer tokens than large language models. Nvidia is pushing into physical AI with a full safety stack for robotaxis and humanoid robots, and Microsoft showed off new AI hardware and more AI built into Windows.

It's been a rough week for Anthropic on safety. One of its models sent a false tip about an unsolved homicide to Philadelphia police back in July, and the company didn't find out for two months. It has now cut its internal evaluations off from the live internet until further notice. A study covered by Ars Technica found AI coding agents write more code, but teams don't ship more software, because human review becomes the bottleneck. And Amazon says it will stop using NDAs in data centre negotiations with local governments, following Microsoft.
